\begin{sectionbox}
Design only. Nothing here is built; the point of writing it first is that one
of the claims in \texttt{README.md} turned out to be wrong as soon as the second
tree was taken seriously, and it is cheaper to find that here.

The brief is in \texttt{PROBLEM.md}. In one line: every citizen votes on every
issue, nobody has time, so a vote is a \emph{delegation} --- refinable issue by
issue, all the way down --- and the finest issues are settled in chat rooms where
a delegate's vote counts once per citizen who sent it to them.
\end{sectionbox}

\section{One container}

\[
  \mathrm{IssueC} \;=\; \mathrm{Issue} \,\triangleleft\, \mathrm{Vote}
\]

A shape is an issue; a position is a valid vote \emph{on that issue}. The
dependency is not decorative. A referendum admits aye or nay, a seat admits a
candidate from a list, a budget line admits a number in a range, and a vote of
the wrong kind is not a dissenting vote but a type error.

\begin{sectionbox}
\textbf{A citizen is a \texttt{Runner<IssueC>}}: somebody an issue can be put to,
who answers. That is the entire ontology.

``Regular citizen'', ``politically active citizen'' and ``political party'' are
not three types. They are three ways of being a runner --- answering from your
own head, answering by asking one other, answering by asking several and
combining. The type cannot tell them apart, and should not, because that is what
makes becoming politically active a decision rather than a migration.
\end{sectionbox}

\section{The two trees}

Both trees carry the same forward map and differ entirely in the backward one.
That is the design.

\subsection*{The delegation tree}

Rooted at a citizen. Its skeleton is the \emph{issue} tree --- government,
then health, education and military, then their sub-issues --- and a delegate is
attached at whichever nodes the citizen has bothered to assign. Everything below
an assignment inherits it until overridden.

\begin{term}
  (citizen)
  |- health ................... party C
  |- education ................ party A
  |    |- early childhood ..... (inherits A)
  |    |- STEM ................ (inherits A)
  |    |- art and creativity .. Sarah Smith      <- overrides, this subtree only
  |    '- physical education .. (inherits A)
  '- military ................. party C
\end{term}

So a delegation is a \textbf{partial function on the issue tree, extended
downward by inheritance}. The total function it denotes is on leaves; the partial
function is its presentation; and the lazy delegation --- party B for everything
--- is the constant function, presented by one assignment at the root.

Exactly one delegate answers any given issue. The backward map is therefore the
\textbf{identity}: what comes back is my vote, unaltered. Delegation is a
\emph{vertical} morphism, which \texttt{federation} calls a degenerate corner of
the formalism. Here the degenerate corner is the subject.

\subsection*{The party tree}

Rooted at a party. Its children are its members, and it too is indexed by issue,
because a party may put defence to one member and health to another.

\begin{term}
  (party A)
  |- education
  |    |- art and creativity .. member: Ali
  |    '- (everything else) ... members: Ali, Bea, Carl   -> amalgamate
  '- health ................... members: Bea, Carl        -> amalgamate
\end{term}

Here several members may answer the same issue, and the party must return
\emph{one} vote. The backward map is therefore an \textbf{amalgamation}: a fold
over the replies --- majority, weighted majority, unanimity, or a party line that
ignores them.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
& delegation tree & party tree \\
\midrule
rooted at & a citizen & a party \\
indexed by & the issue tree & the issue tree \\
forward map & route by issue & route by issue \\
how many answer & exactly one & possibly several \\
\textbf{backward map} & \textbf{identity} & \textbf{a fold} \\
combinator & $+$ & $\boxtimes$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{obs}
The two trees are one construction with two backward maps. This is the same
finding \texttt{federation.pdf} reports for cyclic federations --- ``the
obstruction is in the response direction \dots\ the failure is entirely in the
backward map'' --- arrived at from a different subject. Forward maps are where
the routing lives and they are easy. Backward maps are where the content is.
\end{obs}

\section{The algebra, and all four combinators have a job}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{7.1cm}@{}}
\toprule
& in the algebra & here \\
\midrule
$+$ & disjoint shapes, each keeping its own positions & \textbf{delegation.} One delegate per issue; refinement is which summand \\
$\boxtimes$ & both shapes up front, \textbf{both replies owed} & \textbf{amalgamation.} Every member polled, every reply owed, then folded \\
$\times$ & both offered, \textbf{exactly one answered} & \textbf{abstention.} A second choice, used when the first does not answer \\
$\triangleleft$ & the first reply chooses the second prompt & \textbf{chaining.} You to a party, the party to a member \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

This is the first of these projects in which all four are load-bearing.
\texttt{stalin} and \texttt{federation} never use $+$; \texttt{firm} uses it once.
Here $+$ is the primary structure and $\boxtimes$ is what makes a party a party.

\section{The types}

\begin{term}
// ── Issues ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
type IssueId = string;              // a path: "education/art"
type Kind =
  | { tag: "Binary" }
  | { tag: "Choice"; options: readonly string[] }
  | { tag: "Amount"; lo: number; hi: number };

interface Issue { id: IssueId; kind: Kind; children: readonly IssueId[] }

// ── The container. The position depends on the issue's kind. ──────────
type VoteFor<K extends Kind> =
    K extends { tag: "Binary" } ? { tag: "Aye" } | { tag: "Nay" }
  : K extends { tag: "Choice" } ? { tag: "Pick"; option: string }
  : K extends { tag: "Amount" } ? { tag: "Amount"; value: number }
  : never;

type IssueC =
  | Fib<{ tag: "Poll"; issue: IssueId; visited: readonly VoterId[] }, Ballot>
  | Fib<{ tag: "Reset" }, { tag: "ResetAck" }>;

// A ballot ADMITS FAILURE. Cycles are a reply, not an exception, so the
// caller must handle them and the type says so.
type Ballot =
  | { tag: "Cast";    vote: Vote;   weight: number }
  | { tag: "Cycle";   ring: readonly VoterId[] }
  | { tag: "Abstain"; who: VoterId; why: string };

// ── Delegation: a partial map, inherited downward ─────────────────────
type Delegation = ReadonlyMap<IssueId, VoterId>;

/** The nearest ancestor of `issue`, inclusive, that has an entry. */
function delegateFor(d: Delegation, issue: IssueId): VoterId | null;

// ── Parties ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
type Rule =
  | { tag: "Majority" }                 // one member, one vote
  | { tag: "Weighted" }                 // by delegated weight
  | { tag: "Unanimous" }                // or the party abstains
  | { tag: "Line"; vote: Vote };        // a leaf wearing a party's coat

interface Party { id: VoterId; members: readonly VoterId[]; rule: Rule;
                  assign: Delegation }   // which members get which issues
\end{term}

Two remarks on the types rather than the design.

\texttt{Ballot} admitting \texttt{Cycle} is deliberate. A cycle is an ordinary
outcome of asking a question, not an error condition, and putting it in the
position type forces every caller to say what it does about one.

\texttt{Rule} having a \texttt{Line} constructor is what lets a party be a leaf
without being a different type. A party with a line answers from its own head;
the citizen delegating to it sees the same interface either way, and \emph{ought}
to be able to see which it is --- which is an argument for exposing \texttt{rule},
not for a second type.

\section{Resolution, and a correction}

To resolve a vote is to walk from the citizen, following the forward maps, until
somebody answers. The state --- who has been visited --- rides in the prompt,
because \texttt{federation/mobius.ts} establishes that a header cannot be a type
and \texttt{visited} must be typed if the base case is to be checkable.

\begin{alarmbox}
\textbf{A claim in \texttt{README.md} is wrong, and the party tree is what breaks
it.}

It said: per issue each citizen delegates to at most one representative, so
out-degree is at most one, the graph is \emph{functional}, every component is a
tail running into a single cycle, and cycle detection is correspondingly cheap.

That holds for the delegation tree alone. It fails the moment a party is
involved, because a party polls several members. Out-degree is one at a citizen
and $n$ at a party, so the combined structure is not functional and its
components are not $\rho$-shaped.

\textbf{Resolution is therefore a fold over a tree, not a walk down a path},
cycle detection must be per branch rather than per component, and the same
delegate may be reached twice by different routes --- which is a question about
weights before it is a question about cycles.
\end{alarmbox}

That correction is the reason this document exists before the code does.

\section{Weight}

A delegate's weight on an issue is the number of citizens whose resolution
reaches them on that issue. So weight is an \textbf{output of resolution, not an
input to it}, and it is per-issue: the same delegate carries different weight on
health and on defence.

The re-convergence just noted has a consequence here. If a citizen reaches Ali
directly \emph{and} through party A, Ali must not be counted twice for that
citizen. Weight is therefore a count of \emph{citizens reached}, deduplicated at
the source, rather than a sum accumulated along edges.

\section{What the algebra refuses to decide}

Two choices are political rather than mathematical, and the formalism should
present them rather than make them.

\textbf{Cycle policy.} Detection is settled. What should happen is not. The vote
abstains; or falls back to a $\times$ second choice; or returns to the citizen as
a notification and waits; or is divided evenly round the ring. These are four
different theories of what a delegated vote \emph{is}, and the type
\texttt{Ballot} is deliberately wide enough to express all four.

\textbf{Amalgamation rule.} Whether a party's members are counted one each or by
the weight they personally carry is the difference between a party as a club and
a party as a federation of blocs. \texttt{Rule} makes it a per-party choice, and
it should probably be visible to anyone delegating to that party.

\section{What to build first}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The issue tree, and \texttt{delegateFor} with inheritance. Small, and
  everything rests on it.
\item \texttt{resolve(citizen, issue)} as a tree fold with \texttt{visited} in
  the prompt, returning a \texttt{Ballot}. Cycle policy pluggable, defaulting to
  abstain, so the argument can be had against running code.
\item Weights, deduplicated at the source, and a tally for one leaf issue.
\item Only then: parties as separate processes, one per delegate, and the chat
  room as $\boxtimes$ over the participants.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What is assumed}

That a worker's state may be a set, since \texttt{visited} rides in the prompt
exactly as \texttt{fuel} does in \texttt{mobius}. That question is out to
Strathclyde and unanswered.

That the electorate may be unbounded. The argument is in \texttt{README.md}: every
delegate serves the \emph{same} container, so $\Sigma\,(d : D).\,\mathrm{IssueC}
\cong D \times \mathrm{IssueC}$, and a sum of a constant family is a product with
its index set and does not need the index finite. This is the one place where
voting is easier than the firm, and it is worth knowing what would destroy it:
give parties a capability individuals lack --- a manifesto endpoint, a whip ---
and the family stops being constant.
